    The video-turnaround trick: one prompt, a free 360° character sheet

    The classic character sheet is three or four separate stills: front, back and a face close-up. Each one is a fresh generation, so each one can drift a little. The video-turnaround trick flips this around. You ask a video model for a single slow 360° spin of your character on a plain studio backdrop, then pull every angle you need straight out of the clip. One generation gives you hundreds of frames of the same person, in the same outfit, under the same light. It is one of the cheapest and most useful consistency tricks in AI filmmaking right now.

    • 1Master image to start from: the front view of your character
    • 2Turntable passes: one full body, one head and shoulders
    • 480Frames in this 20-second example, every one a usable angle
    • 8+Clean reference angles harvested, at 45° steps or finer

    Classic sheet vs video turnaround

    Both start from the same character. The classic sheet below gives you a headless front view for the outfit, a back view and two face close-ups. The turnaround clip above gives you everything in between: three-quarter views, both profiles and the back of the head, which are exactly the angles AI video struggles to invent consistently on its own.

    The recipe

    1. Make the master stillGenerate one clean front view: full body, neutral standing pose with the arms slightly away from the body, plain mid-grey backdrop, soft even light. This image decides everything that follows.
    2. Body turntableImage-to-video from the master still, 8 to 10 seconds, locked-off camera. The character rotates on the spot through a full 360° and ends facing the camera again.
    3. Head turntableRepeat from a head-and-shoulders close-up. At full-body scale the face is only a few dozen pixels wide, so a separate close pass keeps the features sharp at every angle.
    4. Harvest the framesScrub the clip, pick the sharpest frame at each angle, upscale it, label it by degree and check it against the master still.

    Prompt templates I use. Keep them boring on purpose: the less happens in the clip, the cleaner the turnaround.

    BODY PASS (image-to-video, 8–10 s, 16:9)

on the spot through one full 360-degree turn at a constant speed, showing the

Plain mid-grey backdrop, soft even light, head and shoulders in frame.

    Pulling the frames takes one command with the free tool FFmpeg. This exports one frame every half second, which at a 10-second turn is roughly every 18°:

    ffmpeg -i turnaround.mp4 -vf fps=2 angle_%02d.png

    How it plugs into different platforms

    Each tool takes references differently, but almost all of them get better the moment you give them more than one angle. The turnaround is the cheapest way to get those angles.

    • Kling 3.0Elements accept up to four reference images, and Kling itself recommends front, side, back and a detail shot. Take them from the turnaround. Kling also accepts a 3 to 8 second single-character video as a reference, and a short turntable clip fits that description exactly.
    • Seedance 2.0 / 2.5Reference slots are limited: four images on some hosts, and only one image alongside a video reference on others. Spend them wisely: the start frame, plus the turnaround angles closest to the camera angles in that shot.
    • Higgsfield Soul IDSoul ID training asks for at least 20 photos of the same person from varied angles and lighting. Turnaround frames cover the angles; add a few shots with different lighting so the identity doesn't learn the studio light. Only train on people you have permission to use.
    • Hailuo / MiniMaxSubject reference works from a single front image, but Hailuo's own guide says three to five views give noticeably better results, and to use a profile reference for side-on shots instead of letting the model guess.
    • Google Flow, Veo and image modelsWhen you build start frames with ChatGPT image or Nano Banana, attach the turnaround angle that matches the pose you want. It beats describing the back of a jacket in words.
    • Open-source (Wan, Flux and LoRAs)Fifteen to thirty turnaround frames make a fast starter dataset for a character LoRA. Caption each one with its angle, and mix in other lighting and expressions so the model doesn't overfit to one grey room.
    • 3D and blockoutsA clean turntable is also good input for image-to-3D tools and photogrammetry. That turns your character into a 3D proxy for a Blender blockout, the method in my Putrajaya Kaiju Showdown case.
    • Your own character sheetsComposite your favourite angles into a clean sheet, or keep them as separate labelled files. Some models copy the layout of a multi-panel sheet into the shot, so single-view files are safer.

    Why it's worth doing

    • One generation, every angle.Every frame comes from the same generation, so the face, the outfit, the proportions and the light can't drift between views the way separate stills do.
    • The model commits to the unseen.The back of the hair, the back of the jacket and the side seams are decided once, in the turnaround. Later shots copy them instead of inventing new ones.
    • Cheap.One or two short clips replace six to eight separate image generations and the re-rolls needed to make them match.
    • Match references to shots.Filming a three-quarter-back shot? Attach the 135° frame. Giving the model the angle it is about to draw is the single biggest consistency gain.
    • A free motion test.You see how the costume, cape or hair behaves when the body turns before you spend credits on real scenes.
    • Approve the design early.A client can sign off the character from every side before production starts, not after the first expensive shot.

    The disadvantages, and how to handle them

    • Softer than a still.Video frames have lower resolution, compression and some motion blur. Pick the sharpest frame near each angle and upscale it before using it as a reference.
    • Uneven spin.In this example the head turns fast through the first half and slower through the second. Don't calculate angles from timestamps; choose them by eye.
    • Invented backs.The model has never seen the back, so it guesses. Logos, tattoos, prints and hairstyles can appear or vanish. Check every view against the master and fix mistakes with inpainting.
    • Asymmetry flips.A scar, a side parting or a chest logo can jump to the wrong side mid-spin. Write asymmetric details into the prompt and check both profiles.
    • The character won't stay still.Some models make the person walk, wave or turn only their head. If that happens, generate two halves with start and end frames: front to back, then back to front.
    • Hands, text and fine detail.Fingers, small accessories and lettering are the first things to warp. Keep hands relaxed and visible, and don't rely on the turnaround for readable text.
    • Video credits cost more than images.A 10-second clip usually costs more than one image, but less than the stack of images and re-rolls it replaces.
    • Likeness and consent.Use this on original characters or on people who have agreed to it. A full 360° of a real person's likeness is exactly what identity tools need, which is a good reason to treat it with care.
    Where it fits in a production

    Master still → video turnaround → labelled angle set → character sheet → start frames → blockout-guided video. The turnaround locks what the character looks like from every side; the blockout locks where they stand and how they move. Together, they remove most of the guesswork that makes AI films look inconsistent.

    01 / The approach

    Five steps, every project

    Whether it's an explainer video, a newsletter or an AI campaign, the work moves through the same five checkpoints.

    1. UnderstandDefine the audience, goal and constraints.
    2. SimplifyFind the core message and give it structure.
    3. CreateCombine hands-on craft with the right AI-assisted tools.
    4. RefineCheck accuracy, accessibility, consistency and relevance.
    5. DeliverPrepare polished assets and workflows for reuse.

    02 / AI generated, human controlled

    AI-powered images, directed by human intent

    AI supports visual production; human judgment determines the purpose, visual direction, selection, refinement and final approval. The process combines structured prompting, controlled experimentation and hands-on image editing to create visuals that serve a clear communication goal, not simply images that look impressive.

    Approach A

    Full AI generation

    The subject, composition, environment, lighting and visual treatment are generated from a complete creative prompt. Human control begins before generation: defining the brief, choosing references, setting constraints and deciding what a successful result must communicate. Outputs are reviewed, corrected and prepared for their use, never accepted automatically.

    Approach B

    Partial AI generation

    AI-generated elements are combined with original photography, illustration, typography, retouching or compositing. AI may supply a background, object or environmental extension while important original elements stay protected. Human editing controls placement, perspective, lighting, colour and integration.

    Which one?

    The brief decides. Full generation suits concept development and purpose-built scenes; partial generation is the right call when the work needs tight control over real products, existing assets, identity or layout.

    01

    Define the communication goal

    Start with the problem the image needs to solve: the message, audience, intended use, format and emotional response. Set practical requirements such as aspect ratio, output size, space for copy and placement across print or digital.

    • Clarify what the viewer should notice, understand or feel.
    • Separate essential requirements from optional exploration.
    • Identify assets or details that must stay accurate and unchanged.
    • Decide whether full generation, partial generation or traditional production fits best.

    Human controlThe creative brief sets the criteria every output is judged against.

    02

    Build the visual direction

    Translate the brief into a deliberate visual language before writing a prompt: subject, setting, composition, camera angle, lighting, palette, materials and degree of realism. References are chosen for specific qualities, not a vague style.

    • Establish the focal point, hierarchy and balance.
    • Define framing, perspective, depth and negative space.
    • Specify lighting direction, softness, contrast and atmosphere.
    • Check brand suitability, cultural context and ethics.

    Human controlArt direction sets the boundaries of exploration.

    03

    Write the master prompt

    Convert the visual direction into a structured prompt with clear priorities, avoiding conflicting requirements and separating non-negotiables from areas where variation is welcome.

    1. Purpose and subject: what the image depicts and needs to communicate.
    2. Environment and composition: setting, framing, placement, perspective, space for text.
    3. Visual treatment: lighting, palette, texture, materials, mood, realism.
    4. Technical requirements: aspect ratio, intended output, platform controls.
    5. Constraints: elements to preserve, unwanted content and errors to avoid.

    Human controlPrompt engineering turns creative intent into explicit, testable instructions.

    04

    Generate controlled variations

    Explore framing, pose, lighting, environment or realism in a controlled sequence, changing one major variable at a time so each decision is easy to evaluate.

    • Keep the brief and essential constraints consistent.
    • Record the model, platform, prompt version and settings.
    • Keep useful alternatives with notes on what changed and why.
    • Use variations to answer creative questions, not to generate endlessly.

    Human controlI decide what to test and when there's enough evidence to move on.

    05

    Select and diagnose

    Compare outputs against the brief, not just visual impact: anatomy, perspective, object structure, lighting, authenticity, brand fit and how it works at its real placement size.

    • Keep: strong composition, atmosphere or details worth preserving.
    • Correct: local problems fixable with targeted generation or editing.
    • Reject: fundamental failures in meaning, accuracy, composition or suitability.

    Human controlCritical evaluation decides what deserves more production.

    06

    Refine, retouch and composite

    Targeted AI editing handles inpainting, outpainting, object replacement or background changes; conventional editing gives precision for masks, typography, colour correction and final compositing.

    1. Prepare the original asset and mark the exact area needing AI help.
    2. Protect elements that must stay unchanged.
    3. Generate the component or extension.
    4. Composite with controlled scale, perspective, edges and depth.
    5. Match light direction, shadows, reflections, colour and texture.
    6. Inspect transitions so everything reads as one image.

    Human controlRetouching and compositing turn an AI output into a finished visual.

    07

    Quality assurance and approval
    • Realism and structure: anatomy, proportions, perspective, shadows, reflections.
    • Consistency: palette, lighting, style and continuity across related outputs.
    • Artefacts: distorted details, repeated textures, halos, awkward edges.
    • Communication: focal point, hierarchy, readability, emotional response.
    • Accessibility: contrast, legibility and alt text.
    • Rights and representation: reference permissions, likeness, misleading implications, disclosure.
    • Output specs: dimensions, crop, resolution, colour and export format.

    Human controlAn attractive output isn't automatically accurate, appropriate or ready to publish. Final approval stays human.

    08

    Document and deliver

    A clear production record explains the decisions behind the result, not just the prompt: goal and audience, approach and why, master prompt and revisions, tools and settings, rejected variations, protected original assets, retouching steps, quality checks and the problem solved.

    Human controlDocumentation makes creative responsibility visible and repeatable.

    Case 01 · Higgsfield Genjutsu

    Sanjini: a Bollywood scene, recast with Higgsfield Genjutsu

    Sanjini restages a busy Mumbai street-market scene from the Bollywood film Ghajini (2008). I kept the original blocking, camera moves and timing, then recast the two leads: my friend Hassan plays the CEO, and the female lead is an imaginary actress I designed from scratch.

    • 2:27Final runtime, English subtitles
    • 5 × 30sSeparate Genjutsu generations, cut together in CapCut
    • 2Leads recast: Hassan and an original AI actress
    • 0Cameras, crews or locations. Every frame is generated.

    How Genjutsu works

    Genjutsu is Higgsfield's video-to-video tool. Most AI video tools invent a shot from a prompt. Genjutsu starts from a clip that already exists, reads how it moves, and rebuilds it with new people, wardrobe or places, while the performance underneath stays the same.

    1. Reference videoUpload one source clip of 4 to 30 seconds. Genjutsu analyses its motion, camera movement, framing and pacing.
    2. Reference imagesAdd up to 30 images that define who and what should appear: faces, outfits, products, locations or a style.
    3. Pick a modeMotion Transfer keeps the movement, camera and timing and rebuilds the cast and the world around it. Object Swap changes one element and leaves the rest of the shot untouched.
    4. Direct and renderAdd a short prompt or a style preset if needed, choose 480p, 720p or 1080p, and generate.
    What's under the hood?

    Higgsfield hasn't said which video model powers Genjutsu. It describes it as its own pipeline: it maps the motion and structure of the source clip, regenerates only the parts you target and locks everything else. Reviewers have compared its output to Seedance 2.5. For Sanjini I used Motion Transfer, so the original performance drives every shot and the faces, clothes and street are rebuilt from my references.

    Casting the leads

    Before generating anything, I built a character sheet for each lead. A sheet locks the face, wardrobe and personality, so the model has one consistent reference to follow.

    Source motion vs. Genjutsu output

    Each frame below is from my final film. The small blurred inset is the matching moment from the source scene, shown only as a motion reference. The walk, the gestures and the camera match it; the people, clothes and street are new.

    Insets: source motion from Ghajini (2008), blurred and credited to its rights holders. They're shown for comparison only.

    Why the scene shifts every 30 seconds

    1. Gen 10:00
    2. Gen 20:30
    3. Gen 31:00
    4. Gen 41:30
    5. Gen 52:00

    Genjutsu accepts up to 30 seconds of source video per generation, so I split the scene into five 30-second parts and generated each one separately. If you watch closely, faces, lighting and street details shift a little at every 30-second mark.

    That's because I didn't use the last frame → first frame continuity method. In that method, the final frame of one generation becomes the starting image of the next, so each block picks up exactly where the last one ended. I skipped it on purpose. Sanjini was an experiment to see how far Genjutsu could go on its own from character sheets alone, and it held up well. In a client version I'd chain the five blocks so the seams disappear.

    Case 02 · Seedance 2.5

    Kisah Nak Beli MC: a 1996 comedy, shot on real streets

    It's 1996. Sam tries to buy a fake MC (medical certificate) for his sister at the neighbourhood clinic, and the doctor answers with a rotan. Sam is played by a younger version of my friend Hassan, his buddy on the roadside is a younger Firdaus, and the doctor is an imaginary character. The streets are real: I rebuilt them from Google Street View.

    • 1996Period setting, for a nostalgic, everyday-Malaysia feel
    • 3 × 30sSeedance 2.5 generations, cut to 1:11 in CapCut
    • 3Characters, each locked with a consistency sheet
    • 4Real Street View shots used as location references

    Character consistency sheets

    The biggest risk in AI drama is face drift: the actor slowly turns into someone else between takes. So before generating anything, I built one consistency sheet per character and attached it to every generation. Each sheet has three panels, and each panel does a specific job.

    1. Front, no headThe full outfit seen from the front, with the head left out on purpose. The model reads the wardrobe without a second face competing with the close-up.
    2. Back viewThe same outfit from behind, for shots where the camera follows a character, such as Sam walking down the lane.
    3. Face close-upOne sharp, evenly lit portrait: the single identity anchor the model matches in every frame.

    All three panels share the same plain grey backdrop and flat studio light, so no location or lighting gets baked into the character. The scene supplies those. Hassan and Firdaus were also aged down to look the way they would have in 1996. Because the same sheets went into all three 30-second generations, the faces hold steady from the first shot to the last.

    Real locations from Google Street View

    For realism, I didn't let the model invent a generic Malaysian street. I took four Street View captures of the real roads around the clinic and used them as location references: terrace houses with zinc and clay-tile roofs, a pink corner house, a roadside warung and an old zinc shack. Seedance rebuilt those streets, and the prompts pushed every frame back to 1996.

    Street View imagery © Google, shown with its original attribution as location reference.

    From three generations to one film

    1. Gen 130s
    2. Gen 230s
    3. Gen 330s

    I generated the drama in three Seedance 2.5 passes of 30 seconds each, using the same three character sheets and the location references every time. In CapCut I cut the 90 seconds down to a tight 1:11 and timed the comedy beats. I added the yellow subtitles, the game-style "Boss Weapon Unlocked: The Rotan of Justice" caption and the sound design.

    Compare this with Sanjini above: there, each 30-second block morphs slightly because nothing tied the generations together. Here, the consistency sheets act as that anchor, so Sam, Firdaus and the doctor stay recognisably the same people across all three generations.

    Case 03 · Seedance 2.5, prompt only

    Cinema Reel With A Twist: four cities, zero location photos

    A travel-vlog love letter that runs from Mumbai to Santorini to a morning run, then turns into a kung-fu fight in a Seoul street-food tent. Every shot comes from Seedance 2.5, and I edited it with Claude. The only images I gave the model were character sheets for the lead. Not a single location reference: every street, sea view and food tent was built from words alone.

    • 2:58A travel reel that ends in a fight
    • 4Settings: Mumbai, Santorini, a park run and Seoul
    • 0Location reference images. Every place comes from the prompt.
    • 4Character sheets keeping one lead consistent across her outfit changes

    Locking the lead first

    The actress had to survive a whole trip: office wear in Mumbai, a sundress in Santorini, athleisure for the run and a white suit for the fight. I made one sheet for her face and hair from every angle, then one turnaround sheet per outfit. With her identity locked by images, every word of the prompt could go into the world around her.

    Realism from words alone: the location playbook

    Without a reference photo, the model has to imagine the place. These are the habits that make it imagine a real place instead of a stock-footage version of one.

    • Name the real place, not a vibe."Marine Drive, Mumbai at sunrise" or "a cliff-top lane in Oia, Santorini" pulls from what the model already knows about those streets. "A seaside city" gives you a generic one.
    • Set the time, weather and light like a cinematographer.Low golden sun from the left, hazy humid morning, blue hour with warm practical bulbs. Light is what makes a frame feel photographed instead of rendered.
    • Plant local, lived-in details.A black-and-yellow Padmini taxi, chai in small glasses, scooters on Santorini steps, plastic stools and steel bowls under a Seoul tent. Three or four true details sell the whole location.
    • Give the background a life.Commuters crossing, a vendor wiping his counter, tourists on a terrace. Extras with a purpose stop the world from looking staged.
    • Speak camera.Handheld phone vlog for the travel parts, then a 35mm anamorphic look with shallow depth of field and natural motion blur for the cinematic beats. Lens language pins down the texture of the footage.
    • Ask for imperfection.Wind in the hair, a slightly blown-out sky, film grain, a little sensor noise at night. Real footage is never perfectly clean.
    • Say what to avoid.No CGI sheen, no plastic skin, no warped signage, no crowd duplication. Negative cues remove the AI look before it appears.
    • Keep the identity out of the prompt.The character sheets carry her face and outfit, so the prompt never spends words on describing her. Every word goes into the place.

    Choreographing the fight

    The twist: she's just trying to eat noodles in peace when a gang of thugs makes a big mistake. An AI fight falls apart fast if you only write "she fights the thugs". Write it the way a stunt coordinator would.

    • A beat sheet, not a sentence.The fight is broken into short timed beats, with one clear action in each: they approach, she puts the chopsticks down, the first strike, the counter, the kick, the reaction, the final pose.
    • Map the space first.Who stands where, which side of the table, which way everyone faces, and how far apart. Fixed screen direction stops fighters from swapping sides between cuts.
    • Use the set as props.The plastic stools, the noodle bowl, the tent pole and the counter give her moves something physical to react to, which makes the hits feel grounded.
    • One attacker at a time.Each thug engages in turn. Two bodies tangling in one frame is where AI creates extra limbs and merged faces.
    • Write the body mechanics.The weight shift before the kick, the pivot on the back foot, the attacker stumbling two steps back and dropping. Cause and effect make the impact read as real.
    • Direct the camera per beat.A wide shot to set up the geography, a handheld follow through the exchange, a speed-ramped slow-motion on the hero kick and quick inserts for impact.
    • Make the outfit a constant.The white-suit sheet keeps her look identical through every cut of the fight, which is the hardest place to keep continuity.
    • Shoot coverage, then cut.Generate more than one take of each beat and keep the cleanest in the edit. The bracketed comedy captions and the hit sounds then land every blow.

    Case 04 · Blender + ChatGPT + Seedance 2.0

    Putrajaya Kaiju Showdown: why AI video should start with a 3D blockout

    A 60-second fight between my original Malaysian superhero and a colossal rock-armoured kaiju at the Putra Mosque in Putrajaya. The monster isn't the point of this project, though. The point is the method: the whole film was directed first as a simple 3D blockout in Blender, and only then handed to AI to make it look real. That one step is what keeps the characters, the location, the scale and the action consistent from shot to shot.

    • 1:01Final runtime, built from four 15-second clips
    • 6Camera shots directed in Blender before any AI was used
    • 1,440Blockout frames, rendered in about 7 minutes at zero credit cost
    • 3×The kaiju's height over the hero, locked in the blockout

    Why the 3D blockout matters

    A blockout is a rough version of the film made from simple shapes: grey mannequins, a proxy monster, the real mosque model, cameras and timing. It looks nothing like the final, and that's the point. It answers every directing question before a single paid generation runs.

    • Consistency is designed, not hoped for.Where each character stands, which way they face, how big they are and where the mosque sits are fixed in 3D. The AI copies a layout instead of inventing a new one every clip.
    • Every shot is directed first.The aerial reveal, the low angle, the orbit around the landing and the handheld fight coverage were all chosen in Blender, where changing a lens or a camera move is free.
    • Mistakes cost nothing to fix.The kaiju was too small and the mosque looked like an island. Both were fixed in minutes in Blender (see below) instead of burning credits on re-generations.
    • It saves real money.An AI generation is the expensive step. With the shots, poses and timing already decided, each clip only has to be generated once or twice instead of being re-rolled until something usable appears.
    • The AI gets one clear job.Not "invent a fight", but "make this exact shot look real". That is the task current video models are best at.
    • Clips that cut together.Because all four clips come from one continuous blockout, the geography and the action flow across the cuts.

    The pipeline

    1. Block out in BlenderJointed mannequins, a proxy kaiju, the Putra Mosque model and its bridge, six cameras and the full 60-second action, rendered as a grey preview film.
    2. Real start framesFor each 15-second clip, the first blockout frame is turned into a photoreal image with ChatGPT, keeping the exact camera angle and layout.
    3. Generate with Seedance 2.0Each clip gets that start frame, a storyboard strip of the blockout poses, hero and kaiju references and a second-by-second prompt.
    4. Edit and compareThe four clips are stitched with matched transitions and a white impact flash, then synced side by side against the blockout.

    Building the blockout

    My first attempt used my detailed Malaysian Super Hero 3D model as the stand-in. It looked finished, but it was one solid statue: no joints, so the arms never moved, and the AI faithfully copied that stiffness. The fix was to go simpler. The hero became a jointed mannequin in his colours, with a cape panel, and the kaiju a jointed proxy with a moving jaw, arms and a five-part tail. Simple shapes that can bend beat a beautiful model that can't.

    The action was then choreographed like a stunt sequence: anticipation before every strike, follow-through after it, a slow and heavy kaiju against a fast hero, and camera shake keyed to every impact. The whole 60 seconds renders in about seven minutes.

    Fixing the film in Blender, not in credits

    Two problems showed up after the first full render, and both were fixed in the blockout before generating again. First, the kaiju and the hero were almost the same height, so the fight read like two wrestlers. Scaling the kaiju to three times the hero changed the choreography too: he now has to slide under its tail and fly up to reach its chest and jaw. Second, the mosque looked like an island in the lake. The real Putra Mosque is joined to the land, so I added a stone causeway bridge with lamp posts.

    Character sheets: locking the look

    The blockout locks where everything is, how big it is and how it moves. The character sheets lock what everything looks like. Each sheet shows the character from the front (from the neck down, so the outfit reads without a second face), from the back, and in a close-up portrait, all on the same plain grey studio backdrop so no lighting or location gets baked in. These designs fed every ChatGPT start frame and were attached as references to every Seedance clip, which is why the hero's armour and cape and the kaiju's horns, rock plates and crystal spines stay the same from the first shot to the last.

    From blockout frame to photoreal start frame

    Each clip starts from a single photoreal frame. I gave ChatGPT the grey blockout frame together with my hero and kaiju references and asked it to keep the exact camera angle, layout and size ratio while turning everything real. Because the blockout already fixed the composition, every start frame lines up with the shot it came from.

    Generating the clips

    Each 15-second clip was generated with four references: the photoreal start frame, a storyboard strip of six blockout poses from that clip, the hero, and the kaiju. The prompt then lists the action second by second, states the 3-to-1 scale and the bridge, and names exactly one creature and one hero.

    • Start with everyone in frame.When a clip began with the hero alone in the sky, the model invented the kaiju itself and twice produced two of them. Starting the finale on a frame that already shows one kaiju fixed it.
    • State the scale in every prompt."A colossal 150-metre giant, three times taller than the hero" keeps the size consistent from clip to clip.
    • Use the right tool for the job.A motion-control model built for one dancing person turned the whole fight into a shirtless man copying the moves. Image-plus-prompt generation guided by the blockout worked far better.
    • Direct the impact.The final kick was written as a slow-motion beat: an energy burst at the boot, a ring shockwave through the clouds, the jaw shattering and glowing cracks spreading across the kaiju.
    Why Seedance 2.0?

    Budget. Seedance 2.0 on DipzStudio generates a 15-second 1080p clip for a fraction of the cost of the newest models, which made a full 60-second film affordable. The trade-off is quality: it is not the highest standard of AI video, and some shots drift from the blockout. This project is for educational purposes only. What it demonstrates is the 3D blockout workflow, which works the same way with any video model, including the most expensive ones.

    Blockout vs final, in sync

    Seedance kept the blockout's story but chose its own timing, for example stretching the kick into a three-second slow-motion moment. I re-timed the blockout beat by beat so both sides hit the rise, the landing, the slide, the punches, the headbutt, the kick and the crash at the same moment.
